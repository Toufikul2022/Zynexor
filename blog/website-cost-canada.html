How much does a website cost in Canada in 2026?

If you run a business in Canada and you are planning a new website, the first question is almost always the same: how much will it cost? The honest answer is that it depends on what the website needs to do. This guide explains what drives the price, the rough ranges you will see quoted in Canada, the Canadian rules that can add work, and how to get a quote you can actually compare.

The short answer

Prices quoted by Canadian freelancers and agencies vary widely, but most projects fall into a few broad bands. Treat these as rough market ranges, not fixed prices:

  • Template or do-it-yourself site: from a few hundred dollars up to about $3,000 CAD, plus monthly platform fees. Good for testing an idea quickly.
  • Custom small-business website (5 to 15 pages): commonly around $5,000 to $15,000 CAD. Custom design, basic search optimisation, contact and booking forms.
  • Content-rich or lead-generation website: often $15,000 to $40,000 CAD or more. Many service pages, a blog, integrations with a CRM or booking system, careful conversion design.
  • E-commerce stores and web applications: usually priced higher again, because product catalogues, payments, user accounts and dashboards take far more design and development time.

What actually drives the price

Two websites with the same number of pages can have very different prices. These are the factors that matter most:

  • Custom design versus a template. A template is faster and cheaper. Custom design costs more but is built around your customers and your brand, which usually converts better.
  • Content. Writing clear, search-friendly copy for every page is real work. If you supply the text, the price drops. If the agency writes it, budget for it.
  • Functionality. Online booking, payments, client portals, multilingual content, live data tables and CRM integrations each add time.
  • Number of unique page layouts. Twenty pages that share three layouts cost less than ten pages that are all different.
  • Speed, accessibility and SEO. Doing these properly from the start costs a little more and saves a lot later.

Canadian requirements that can add scope

A few Canadian rules are worth planning for early. This is general information, not legal advice, so check the details for your business with a lawyer.

  • French content. If you serve customers in Quebec, Quebec's language laws generally require your commercial website to be available in French. A bilingual site is a bigger project than an English-only one.
  • Accessibility (AODA). In Ontario, many organisations with 50 or more employees must make their public websites meet WCAG 2.0 Level AA. Read our guide to AODA, ADA and WCAG.
  • Privacy (PIPEDA). If your site collects personal information through forms or analytics, you need a clear privacy policy and sensible data handling.
  • Anti-spam (CASL). If you plan to send marketing emails, your sign-up forms need proper consent.

Ongoing costs to budget for

The build is only part of the picture. Plan for a domain name (usually tens of dollars per year), hosting or a platform subscription, software licences or plugins, and maintenance: updates, backups, security fixes and small content changes. Many businesses also pay for ongoing SEO, content or advertising once the site is live.

Fixed price or monthly retainer?

When the scope is clear, a fixed price gives you certainty. When you want continuous improvement, such as new landing pages, campaigns and content every month, a retainer is usually better value. A good agency will tell you which suits your situation and will write the scope down in plain language.

How to get quotes you can compare

  • Describe your goal, not just the deliverable. "I want more booked consultations" leads to a better proposal than "I need a five-page website".
  • List must-have features, such as booking, payments, French content or a CRM link.
  • Share two or three websites you like and say why.
  • Ask every agency what is included: copywriting, photography, SEO set-up, accessibility, training and post-launch support.
  • Ask who owns the design files and code when the project ends. The answer should be you.

FAQ

How long does it take to build a website in Canada?

A brand-new small-business website usually takes six to ten weeks from kick-off to launch. Simple template sites can go live faster; larger content or e-commerce sites take longer.

Do I need a bilingual English and French website?

If you sell to customers in Quebec, Quebec's language rules generally require French content on commercial websites. Outside Quebec it is a business choice. Check your obligations with a lawyer.

Are website design services taxable in Canada?

Website design is generally a taxable service, so expect GST or HST (and PST where it applies) on top of the quoted price, depending on your province and the supplier's registration.
