Website accessibility in Canada and the USA: AODA, ADA and WCAG explained

Accessible websites work for everyone, including people who use screen readers, keyboards instead of a mouse, or zoomed-in text. In Canada and the USA, accessibility is also a legal topic. This guide explains the rules in plain language for business owners. It is general information, not legal advice.

WCAG: the technical standard behind the laws

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are published by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C). They describe how to make content perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. Each guideline has three levels: A, AA and AAA. Level AA is the target most laws and policies point to. The main versions are WCAG 2.0 (2008), 2.1 (2018) and 2.2 (2023); newer versions add requirements and stay compatible with older ones.

Canada: AODA and the Accessible Canada Act

  • Ontario (AODA). Under the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act, public sector organisations and private or non-profit organisations with 50 or more employees in Ontario must make their public websites and web content conform to WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with limited exceptions such as live captions and pre-recorded audio description.
  • Federally regulated organisations. The Accessible Canada Act applies to the federal government and federally regulated sectors such as banking, telecommunications and transportation, with requirements set through regulations and standards.
  • Other provinces such as Manitoba and Nova Scotia have their own accessibility laws with standards being phased in.

USA: the ADA and Section 508

  • ADA Title III covers businesses that are "places of public accommodation". The Department of Justice has said that businesses' websites should be accessible, and website accessibility lawsuits against private businesses are common. WCAG 2.1 Level AA is the benchmark usually referenced.
  • ADA Title II covers state and local governments. A 2024 DOJ rule adopted WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the technical standard for their websites and apps, with compliance deadlines phased in by size.
  • Section 508 applies to federal agencies and the technology they buy.

A practical checklist for your website

  • Give every image a meaningful alternative text, or an empty one if it is decorative.
  • Make sure text has enough colour contrast against its background.
  • Everything must work with a keyboard, with a visible focus outline.
  • Use real headings in order, so screen-reader users can navigate.
  • Label every form field and explain errors clearly.
  • Add captions to videos with speech, and avoid content that flashes.
  • Let people pause or stop animations and respect the "reduce motion" setting.
  • Test with an automated checker, then with a keyboard and a screen reader.

Why accessibility is good business

Accessible sites are easier for everyone to use, load cleanly on more devices and are easier for search engines to understand. They also reduce legal risk and show customers that you care about them.

FAQ

Does my small business website need to be accessible?

In Ontario, organisations with 50 or more employees have specific AODA website requirements. In the US, businesses open to the public face ADA risk if their website is inaccessible. Even where no rule applies, accessibility helps customers and SEO.

Which WCAG level should I aim for?

Aim for WCAG 2.1 Level AA, or 2.2 Level AA for new projects. It meets the commonly referenced standards in both countries.

Will an accessibility plugin or overlay make my site compliant?

Overlays alone rarely fix underlying problems such as missing labels, poor structure or keyboard traps. Fixing the site itself is the reliable approach.
